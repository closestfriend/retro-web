Artist Statement

I do not merely take photographs. I receive them.

For the past four years, I have walked the streets of this city—its forgotten corners, its liminal spaces, its margins where humanity reveals its unvarnished truth. I am drawn to the spaces between intention and accident, between the posed and the authentic, between what we show the world and what we cannot hide.

My work is an ongoing meditation on the human condition as manifested in urban space. The bus stop. The diner window. The alleyway at dusk. These are the theaters where the drama of existence unfolds, unscripted and unrehearsed.

"To photograph is to hold one's breath, when all faculties converge to capture fleeting reality. It's at that precise moment that mastering an image becomes a great physical and intellectual joy."
— Henri Cartier-Bresson

I seek what Cartier-Bresson called the decisive moment—that fraction of a second when form and content align to reveal something essential about our shared humanity. It cannot be manufactured. It can only be witnessed by one who has learned to see.

My images are not documents. They are poems written in silver and light.

— M. Blackwell
October 1998

About the Artist

Based in: Pacific Northwest, USA
Active since: 1994
Primary medium: 35mm film, silver gelatin prints
Influences: Henri Cartier-Bresson, Robert Frank, Garry Winogrand, Eugene Atget

Marcus Blackwell (b. 1965) began his photographic journey in the summer of 1994, after discovering a vintage Leica M3 at an estate sale. What began as a casual interest quickly became an obsession—and eventually, a calling.

After years of studying the masters—poring over the pages of The Americans, The Decisive Moment, and countless issues of Aperture—Blackwell developed his distinctive approach to urban documentary photography. His work has been described as "hauntingly intimate" and "unflinchingly honest."

Blackwell maintains a home darkroom where all silver gelatin prints are hand-processed using traditional techniques. He is a firm believer in the irreplaceable quality of film-based photography, though he has recently begun exploring digital capture as a supplementary medium (see Film vs. Digital).

Current Projects:
• "Nocturnes" — A long-term study of urban spaces between midnight and dawn
• "The Waiting" — Exploring liminal moments in public transportation
• Ongoing documentation of warehouse district gentrification

Mr. Blackwell is available for select commissioned work and portfolio reviews. He does not photograph weddings, bar mitzvahs, or corporate events.

Philosophy of the Image

On the Nature of Street Photography

The street photographer is not a hunter. The street photographer is a witness.

I reject the notion of photography as predation—the "capturing" of unwilling subjects, the "shooting" of strangers. Instead, I understand my practice as one of profound attention. I do not take from the world; I receive what the world offers to those patient enough to see.

"Photography is not about the thing photographed. It is about how that thing looks photographed."
— Garry Winogrand

On the Decisive Moment

Cartier-Bresson's concept of l'instant décisif remains the cornerstone of my practice. There exists, in every scene, a singular moment when all elements align—gesture, light, geometry, meaning. The photographer's task is to recognize this moment and to have the technical mastery to record it.

This cannot be taught. It can only be cultivated through years of practice, thousands of frames, countless hours of walking and watching and waiting.

On Urban Poetry

The city speaks to those who know how to listen. Every cracked sidewalk, every steam vent, every rain-slicked street is a verse in an endless poem. My photographs are translations of this poem—imperfect, necessarily incomplete, but faithful to the original.

I am not interested in beauty for its own sake. I am interested in truth—and truth, as we know, is rarely beautiful in the conventional sense. It is something harder and more valuable: it is authentic.

"The world just does not fit conveniently into the format of a 35mm camera."
— W. Eugene Smith

A Note on Equipment:

I am often asked what camera I recommend for beginners. My answer is always the same: any camera you have. A Leica will not make you a photographer any more than a Steinway will make you a pianist. Vision cannot be purchased.

That said, there is something to be learned from using professional tools. The Leica M system forces a certain discipline, a certain intimacy with the subject. But this is not magic—it is simply ergonomics in service of seeing.

The Digital Question

Personal reflections on the changing landscape of photography

I must confess: I am conflicted.

Earlier this year, I acquired a Kodak DC260—one of the new breed of "serious" digital cameras. My intention was purely academic: to understand this technology, to evaluate its potential (or lack thereof) for fine art photography.

What I found surprised me.

On the one hand, the immediate feedback is... seductive. To see an image moments after capture, to evaluate composition and exposure without waiting for contact sheets—this has pedagogical value. I will admit this.

But.

The images themselves are fundamentally different from those produced by silver halide. There is a clinical quality, a certain soullessness that I cannot quite articulate. The dynamic range is limited. The files are crude mosaics of pixels rather than the infinite, organic gradations of grain.

Why Film Will Endure

  1. Tonality: Silver gelatin prints possess a luminosity that inkjet cannot replicate. The paper glows from within.
  2. Archival permanence: A properly processed silver print will outlast any digital file format. In 100 years, who will read a JPEG?
  3. The discipline of limitation: 36 exposures forces intentionality. When every frame costs money and time, you learn to see before you shoot.
  4. The darkroom as meditation: There is something sacred about working in red light, watching an image emerge from nothingness. This cannot be replicated by clicking "Print."
  5. Authenticity: A film negative is a physical artifact, a direct index of light reflected from the subject. A digital file is merely an interpretation, a mathematical approximation.

A Reluctant Concession

And yet... I am not a Luddite. I recognize that technology evolves. The early daguerreotypists surely sneered at the calotype, and look where we are now.

I will continue my experiments with the DC260. Perhaps there are applications where digital capture makes sense—documentation, commercial work (not that I do commercial work), web presentation. But for my art? For the work that matters?

Film. Always film.

A Prediction:

Mark my words: digital photography will find its niche, primarily in journalism and commercial applications where speed trumps quality. But it will never replace film for fine art photography. The aesthetic difference is too profound. Collectors want silver, not ink.

In ten years, we will look back on this "digital revolution" as a passing fad, much like instant photography before it.

— Written November 1998
